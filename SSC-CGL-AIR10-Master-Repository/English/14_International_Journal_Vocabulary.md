## What this is
A set of **298 higher-level words** drawn from the register of **international newspapers (The Economist, The Guardian, NYT), scientific journals (Nature, The Lancet), and global reports (UN, IMF, World Bank)**.

This is a **step above** the Indian-newspaper editorial vocabulary in your other files. These are the words that make academic and international writing feel "hard" — once you own them, serious English opens up. **None repeat** the words in [12_Newspaper_Vocabulary_1_Year.md](12_Newspaper_Vocabulary_1_Year.md) or the starter list.

### ⚠️ One honest note
I cannot read live journals or papers — but these sources reuse a stable, well-known pool of analytical vocabulary, and that is exactly what this list captures. Every word here is a real, current word used in that register.

## 🔬 Science & Research Terms  *(63 words)*

*Words from scientific papers, journals (Nature, The Lancet) and any data-driven report. Learn these to read research and statistics.*

**Empirical** *(adjective)* — based on real observation, testing, or experience - not just on ideas or guessing · **easy synonyms:** tested, observed, real-world · 🧠 **trick:** 'Empirical' sounds like 'experience' - knowledge from real experience, not theory.
> *The study offers empirical evidence that early schooling improves later income, drawn from data on 50,000 children.*

**Hypothesis** *(noun)* — an idea or guess that you test to see if it is true · **easy synonyms:** guess, idea, theory · 🧠 **trick:** 'Hypo' = under/before - a hypothesis comes BEFORE the proof.
> *Researchers tested the hypothesis that warmer oceans speed up the spread of the disease.*

**Correlation** *(noun)* — a link where two things change together (when one goes up, the other goes up or down) · **easy synonyms:** link, connection, relationship · 🧠 **trick:** 'Co-relation' = two things related together, moving as a pair.
> *The paper found a strong correlation between air pollution and hospital visits for asthma.*

**Variable** *(noun)* — a factor that can change and is measured in a study · **easy synonyms:** factor, element, condition · 🧠 **trick:** 'Vary-able' = something able to vary (change).
> *Income, age, and diet were the main variables the scientists tracked over ten years.*

**Sample** *(noun)* — a small group chosen to stand for a much larger group · **easy synonyms:** group, selection, portion · 🧠 **trick:** A food 'sample' is a small taste of the whole dish.
> *The survey used a sample of 2,000 adults to represent the whole country.*

**Data** *(noun)* — facts, numbers, or information collected for study · **easy synonyms:** facts, figures, information · 🧠 **trick:** 'Data' = the dots of information you collect, then connect.
> *The data showed that vaccination rates rose sharply after the campaign began.*

**Evidence** *(noun)* — facts or signs that show something is true · **easy synonyms:** proof, facts, signs · 🧠 **trick:** 'E-VIDE-nce' - you can VIEW (vid = see) the proof.
> *There is growing evidence that the new drug lowers blood pressure in older patients.*

**Measure** *(verb)* — to find out the size, amount, or level of something · **easy synonyms:** check, count, size up · 🧠 **trick:** A tape 'measure' tells you exactly how much.
> *The team used satellites to measure the rate of ice loss in Antarctica.*

**Analyse** *(verb)* — to study something carefully to understand its parts and meaning · **easy synonyms:** study, examine, break down · 🧠 **trick:** 'Analyse' = break apart (loosen) to see what is inside.
> *Scientists analysed blood samples from thousands of patients to find the pattern.*

**Survey** *(noun)* — a set of questions asked to many people to gather information · **easy synonyms:** poll, study, questionnaire · 🧠 **trick:** 'Sur-vey' = to view (vey) over (sur) a whole group.
> *A nationwide survey found that two in three workers want to keep working from home.*

**Average** *(noun)* — the middle or typical amount, found by adding values and dividing · **easy synonyms:** typical, middle, mean · 🧠 **trick:** 'Average' = the all-round middle of a set of numbers.
> *On average, patients recovered two days faster with the new treatment.*

**Factor** *(noun)* — one thing that helps cause or shape a result · **easy synonyms:** cause, reason, part · 🧠 **trick:** A 'factor' is one part that goes into a 'fact' (result).
> *Diet and exercise were the two biggest factors linked to a longer life.*

**Significant** *(adjective)* — large or important enough to matter, especially in results · **easy synonyms:** important, large, notable · 🧠 **trick:** 'Signi-ficant' - it sends a strong signal that matters.
> *The drop in infections was statistically significant, the Lancet study reported.*

**Random** *(adjective)* — chosen by chance, with no set order or pattern · **easy synonyms:** by chance, mixed, unplanned · 🧠 **trick:** 'Random' = ran any direction - no plan, pure chance.
> *In a random trial, half the patients were given the drug and half a placebo.*

**Control** *(noun)* — a group in a test that gets no treatment, used to compare results · **easy synonyms:** comparison group, baseline · 🧠 **trick:** The 'control' group is your steady measuring stick.
> *Compared with the control group, the treated patients showed clear improvement.*

**Outcome** *(noun)* — the result or effect that comes out of something · **easy synonyms:** result, effect, end · 🧠 **trick:** 'Out-come' = what comes out at the end.
> *Better health outcomes were seen in regions with more clean drinking water.*

**Observe** *(verb)* — to watch carefully and notice what happens · **easy synonyms:** watch, notice, see · 🧠 **trick:** 'Observe' - keep an eye on, and serve it your full attention.
> *Astronomers observed the distant star dimming over several weeks.*

**Indicate** *(verb)* — to show or point to something · **easy synonyms:** show, point to, suggest · 🧠 **trick:** An 'indicator' light points to (indicates) what's happening.
> *Early results indicate that the vaccine is safe for young children.*

**Baseline** *(noun)* — a starting level used to measure later change · **easy synonyms:** starting point, base, level · 🧠 **trick:** 'Base-line' = the bottom line you start measuring from.
> *Doctors recorded each patient's baseline weight before the diet began.*

**Ratio** *(noun)* — a way of comparing two amounts by how many times one fits the other · **easy synonyms:** proportion, balance, rate · 🧠 **trick:** 'Ratio' = the rate at which two things compare.
> *The study reported a ratio of three women to every two men in the workforce.*

**Yield** *(verb)* — to produce or give a result or amount · **easy synonyms:** produce, give, return · 🧠 **trick:** A field 'yields' a crop - it gives back what you put in.
> *The new farming method yielded twenty percent more grain per acre.*

**Replicate** *(verb)* — to repeat a study or test to check if the result is the same · **easy synonyms:** repeat, copy, redo · 🧠 **trick:** 'Re-plic-ate' = to fold/copy again (like a replica).
> *Other labs failed to replicate the original findings, raising doubts about the claim.*

**Robust** *(adjective)* — strong and reliable; holds true even when tested in different ways · **easy synonyms:** strong, solid, sturdy · 🧠 **trick:** Sounds like 'robot' - strong, tough, and hard to break.
> *The link between smoking and lung cancer is robust and has been confirmed in study after study.*

**Marginal** *(adjective)* — very small; only just big enough to notice · **easy synonyms:** tiny, slight, small · 🧠 **trick:** A 'margin' is the thin edge of a page - marginal means a thin, small amount.
> *The vaccine showed only a marginal benefit in older patients, the researchers cautioned.*

**Threshold** *(noun)* — the point at which something starts to happen or change · **easy synonyms:** limit, starting point, boundary · 🧠 **trick:** A 'threshold' is the doorstep you cross to enter - the point where you step over.
> *Temperatures are nearing the threshold beyond which coral reefs cannot recover.*

**Plateau** *(noun)* — a level where something stops rising and stays flat · **easy synonyms:** flat stage, level off, steady point · 🧠 **trick:** A plateau is a flat-topped mountain - the line goes up then stays flat on top.
> *After years of rapid growth, vaccination rates have hit a plateau across the region.*

**Attenuate** *(verb)* — to make weaker or smaller · **easy synonyms:** weaken, reduce, lessen · 🧠 **trick:** 'a-thin-uate' - to make something thin and weak.
> *The mask did not stop infection entirely but attenuated the spread of the virus.*

**Projection** *(noun)* — a forecast of what a number will be in the future · **easy synonyms:** forecast, prediction, outlook · 🧠 **trick:** A film 'projector' throws an image forward - a projection throws numbers into the future.
> *IMF projections suggest the economy will grow by three percent next year.*

**Margin of error** *(noun)* — the small range a result may be off by · **easy synonyms:** allowance, give-or-take, range · 🧠 **trick:** The 'margin' (edge) where 'error' (mistake) might hide - the give-or-take zone.
> *The poll showed 52 percent support, with a margin of error of three points.*

**Outlier** *(noun)* — a result that is very different from all the others · **easy synonyms:** odd one out, exception, stray result · 🧠 **trick:** It 'lies out' - sits far outside the main group of points.
> *One outlier showed a huge effect, but the rest of the data was flat.*

**Magnitude** *(noun)* — the size or scale of something · **easy synonyms:** size, scale, amount · 🧠 **trick:** Earthquake 'magnitude' tells you how big it was - magnitude = bigness.
> *The magnitude of the temperature rise surprised even the most senior climate scientists.*

**Consistent** *(adjective)* — staying the same; not changing from test to test · **easy synonyms:** steady, even, dependable · 🧠 **trick:** 'con-sist' - it keeps standing the same way each time.
> *The results were consistent across all three independent laboratories.*

**Negligible** *(adjective)* — so small it can be safely ignored · **easy synonyms:** tiny, unimportant, barely there · 🧠 **trick:** 'neglect-ible' - small enough that you can neglect (ignore) it.
> *The added risk from the chemical was found to be negligible at normal doses.*

**Pronounced** *(adjective)* — very clear and strong; easy to notice · **easy synonyms:** strong, marked, obvious · 🧠 **trick:** Like a word 'pronounced' loudly - a pronounced effect stands out clearly.
> *The effect was most pronounced among children under the age of five.*

**Linear** *(adjective)* — changing at a steady, straight-line rate · **easy synonyms:** straight, even, steady · 🧠 **trick:** 'line-ar' - it follows a straight line on the graph.
> *The relationship between dose and effect was roughly linear up to a point.*

**Deviation** *(noun)* — how far a value moves away from the usual or expected one · **easy synonyms:** difference, drift, gap · 🧠 **trick:** 'de-via' (off the road, via=way) - it strays off the expected path.
> *A small deviation from the normal range was recorded in the final week.*

**Corroborate** *(verb)* — to give extra evidence that supports an idea or claim · **easy synonyms:** confirm, back up, prove · 🧠 **trick:** co-ROB-orate = two robbers giving the SAME alibi to back each other up.
> *Independent satellite data corroborate the team's finding that the glacier is melting faster than predicted.*

**Discrete** *(adjective)* — separate and individually distinct · **easy synonyms:** separate, distinct, apart · 🧠 **trick:** disCRETE — note the 'e' at the end; think 'sepa-RATE' = kept apart (not 'discreet' = secretive).
> *Researchers identified four discrete stages in the disease's progression.*

**Extrapolate** *(verb)* — to use known data to estimate something beyond it · **easy synonyms:** predict, estimate, project · 🧠 **trick:** extra-POL-ate = stretch the line EXTRA far beyond the dots you have.
> *From current trends, economists extrapolate that the debt will double within fifteen years.*

**Manifest** *(verb)* — to show or appear clearly · **easy synonyms:** show, appear, reveal · 🧠 **trick:** MANIFEST = made so plain it could be on a public 'manifesto'.
> *The disease often manifests as fatigue long before other symptoms emerge.*

**Postulate** *(verb)* — to suggest an idea as a starting point, before it is proven · **easy synonyms:** suggest, propose, assume · 🧠 **trick:** 'postulate' = to POST (put up) an idea on the board for discussion.
> *The authors postulate that warming oceans are driving the change in fish migration.*

**Resilience** *(noun)* — the ability to recover quickly after a shock or disaster · **easy synonyms:** toughness, bounce-back, strength · 🧠 **trick:** 're-sili' sounds like 're-spring' -> a spring bounces back.
> *The IMF stressed building economic resilience so countries can withstand future climate shocks.*

**Sustainable** *(adjective)* — able to continue for a long time without using up resources or harming the planet · **easy synonyms:** lasting, green, long-term · 🧠 **trick:** 'sustain' = keep going -> something you can keep doing forever.
> *The UN's Sustainable Development Goals aim to end poverty by 2030 without damaging the environment.*

**Mitigation** *(noun)* — action taken to make a problem less serious · **easy synonyms:** easing, reducing, softening · 🧠 **trick:** 'mit' sounds like 'mitt' (glove) -> a glove softens a hard catch.
> *Climate mitigation focuses on cutting emissions, while adaptation prepares us for warming already locked in.*

**Adaptation** *(noun)* — changing in order to cope with new conditions · **easy synonyms:** adjustment, change, coping · 🧠 **trick:** 'adapt' -> adjust yourself to fit new surroundings.
> *Coastal cities are spending billions on adaptation measures such as sea walls against rising tides.*

**Intervention** *(noun)* — an action taken to change or improve a situation · **easy synonyms:** action, step in, help · 🧠 **trick:** 'inter-vene' = come between -> step in to change things.
> *Early health interventions, such as vaccines, save millions of children each year.*

**Prevalence** *(noun)* — how common or widespread something is · **easy synonyms:** commonness, spread, how widespread · 🧠 **trick:** 'prevail' -> what prevails (rules) everywhere is prevalent.
> *The study found a rising prevalence of diabetes among young adults in cities.*

**Cumulative** *(adjective)* — increasing by being added together step by step · **easy synonyms:** growing, adding up, total · 🧠 **trick:** 'accumulate' -> a heap that keeps piling up.
> *The cumulative effect of small yearly emissions has been a sharp rise in sea levels.*

**Heuristic** *(noun)* — a quick rule of thumb that helps you solve a problem fast, even if not perfectly · **easy synonyms:** shortcut, rough rule, quick method · 🧠 **trick:** Sounds like 'eureka!' - a HEURISTIC is a quick trick to find an answer.
> *Investors often rely on a simple heuristic rather than analysing every data point.*

**Paradox** *(noun)* — something that seems to contradict itself but may still be true · **easy synonyms:** contradiction, puzzle, strange truth · 🧠 **trick:** PARA (against) + DOX (opinion) - it goes against what you'd expect.
> *The study highlights the paradox of choice: more options can leave people less happy.*

**Systemic** *(adjective)* — affecting a whole system, not just one part; built into how things work · **easy synonyms:** system-wide, deep-rooted, whole-system · 🧠 **trick:** SYSTEM-ic - it runs through the entire SYSTEM.
> *The crash exposed systemic weaknesses in the global banking system.*

**Endemic** *(adjective)* — regularly found in a certain place or group; a constant, settled presence · **easy synonyms:** always present, native, built-in · 🧠 **trick:** EN (in) + DEMIC (people) - settled IN a people or place.
> *Corruption had become endemic in the country's police force.*

**Inconclusive** *(adjective)* — not giving a clear answer or final result · **easy synonyms:** unclear, undecided, open · 🧠 **trick:** in + conclusive — NOT a clear conclusion.
> *The early findings were inconclusive, so the team called for further research.*

**Correlate** *(verb)* — to have a connection where things change together · **easy synonyms:** link, match, relate · 🧠 **trick:** co + relate — two things relate and move together.
> *Higher pollution levels correlated strongly with rising rates of asthma.*

**Anomaly** *(noun)* — something that is unusual and does not fit the pattern · **easy synonyms:** oddity, exception, irregularity · 🧠 **trick:** A-NO-MALY — NOT normal, the odd one out.
> *The satellite recorded a temperature anomaly far above the seasonal norm.*

**Confound** *(verb)* — to confuse results by mixing in a hidden cause · **easy synonyms:** confuse, muddle, mislead · 🧠 **trick:** CON-FOUND — a found-out hidden factor that confuses.
> *Smoking may confound the link between coffee and heart disease.*

**Uncertainty** *(noun)* — the range of doubt in a measurement or result · **easy synonyms:** doubt, unsureness, error · 🧠 **trick:** un + CERTAIN — not certain, has a margin of doubt.
> *The forecast carried a high degree of uncertainty about future warming.*

**Pandemic** *(noun)* — A disease outbreak that spreads across many countries or the whole world · **easy synonyms:** global outbreak, plague, epidemic · 🧠 **trick:** pan = all, demic = people; a disease hitting everyone.
> *The Lancet study modelled how quickly a new pandemic could spread.*

**Contiguous** *(adjective)* — touching or next to each other · **easy synonyms:** touching, neighbouring, joined · 🧠 **trick:** con-TIG = things TOUCH side by side.
> *The disease spread quickly across contiguous districts.*

**Adjacent** *(adjective)* — lying next to something · **easy synonyms:** next to, nearby, neighbouring · 🧠 **trick:** ADJACENT = A-D-JACK sits next to you.
> *Flooding affected the river and all adjacent farmland.*

**Prospective** *(adjective)* — likely to happen or be in the future · **easy synonyms:** future, expected, possible · 🧠 **trick:** PRO-SPECT = looking forward to PROSPECTS.
> *The trial followed prospective patients over five years.*

**Preliminary** *(adjective)* — coming before the main part; early · **easy synonyms:** early, first, initial · 🧠 **trick:** PRE-lim = comes BEFORE the limit/main work.
> *Preliminary results suggest the vaccine is safe.*

**Synthesis** *(noun)* — combining parts into a whole · **easy synonyms:** blend, mixture, combination · 🧠 **trick:** SYNthesis = SYNC pieces into one.
> *The review offers a synthesis of fifty separate studies.*

## 🎓 Academic / Journal Register  *(102 words)*

*The formal verbs and adjectives of serious analytical writing — the "voice" of scholarly articles and essays.*

**Derive** *(verb)* — to get or work out something from a source · **easy synonyms:** get from, draw from, obtain · 🧠 **trick:** 'De-rive' - pull it out, like water from a river (rive).
> *The figures were derived from census records going back fifty years.*

**Assess** *(verb)* — to judge the value, size, or quality of something · **easy synonyms:** judge, check, rate · 🧠 **trick:** 'Assess' sounds like 'a-sess' - sit and size it up carefully.
> *The World Bank assessed the damage from the floods at over two billion dollars.*

**Discernible** *(adjective)* — able to be seen or noticed, if you look carefully · **easy synonyms:** noticeable, visible, clear · 🧠 **trick:** From 'discern' (to notice) - a discernible change is one you can notice.
> *There was no discernible difference between the treated and untreated groups.*

**Incremental** *(adjective)* — happening in small steps rather than all at once · **easy synonyms:** step-by-step, gradual, slow · 🧠 **trick:** 'increment' = one small step up; incremental means step by step.
> *Progress on the disease has been incremental, with each study adding a little.*

**Aggregate** *(adjective)* — combined or added together into one total · **easy synonyms:** total, combined, whole · 🧠 **trick:** To 'aggregate' is to gather into a group - the grand total.
> *The aggregate effect of small savings across millions of homes was huge.*

**Infer** *(verb)* — to work out a conclusion from the evidence · **easy synonyms:** conclude, work out, gather · 🧠 **trick:** 'in-fer' (to carry in) - you carry a conclusion in from the clues.
> *From the fossil record, scientists infer that the climate was once far warmer.*

**Plausible** *(adjective)* — believable; reasonable enough to be true · **easy synonyms:** believable, likely, reasonable · 🧠 **trick:** 'plaus' sounds like 'applause' - an idea worth clapping for because it makes sense.
> *The authors offer a plausible explanation for the sudden fall in cases.*

**Underlying** *(adjective)* — hidden beneath the surface; the real cause behind something · **easy synonyms:** hidden, basic, root · 🧠 **trick:** It 'lies under' the visible problem - the real thing beneath.
> *The study aimed to find the underlying cause of the recurring infections.*

**Diminishing returns** *(noun)* — the point where extra effort or input gives less and less benefit · **easy synonyms:** less and less gain, smaller payoff, fading benefit · 🧠 **trick:** Each extra spoonful of sugar in tea adds less sweetness - returns shrink.
> *After a certain dose, the drug showed diminishing returns and no extra effect.*

**Posit** *(verb)* — to put forward an idea as true so it can be discussed or tested · **easy synonyms:** suggest, claim, assume · 🧠 **trick:** POSIT = to POSITion an idea on the table for others to look at.
> *The authors posit that rising sea temperatures, rather than overfishing, are the main driver of the decline in cod stocks.*

**Delineate** *(verb)* — to describe or mark out something clearly and in detail · **easy synonyms:** outline, describe, draw · 🧠 **trick:** de-LINE-ate = to draw clear LINEs around an idea.
> *The report delineates three possible pathways by which the country could reach net-zero emissions by 2050.*

**Underpin** *(verb)* — to support or form the base of something · **easy synonyms:** support, back, hold up · 🧠 **trick:** UNDER-PIN = a PIN placed UNDER something to hold it up.
> *Strong institutions and the rule of law underpin long-term economic growth, the World Bank study argues.*

**Salient** *(adjective)* — most important or most noticeable · **easy synonyms:** key, main, standing out · 🧠 **trick:** SALIENT sounds like 'sail' — a sail is the part of a boat that stands out most.
> *The minister summarised the salient points of the agreement before the press conference began.*

**Cogent** *(adjective)* — clear, logical and convincing · **easy synonyms:** strong, clear, convincing · 🧠 **trick:** COGENT shares 'cog' with 'cognition' (thinking) — a well-thought-out point.
> *The defence presented a cogent argument that the evidence had been gathered unlawfully.*

**Converse** *(noun)* — the opposite of a statement; the reverse · **easy synonyms:** opposite, reverse, flip side · 🧠 **trick:** con-VERSE = the verse turned the other way round.
> *The data show that wealth improves health, but the converse — that good health builds wealth — also holds.*

**Preclude** *(verb)* — to prevent something from happening; to rule it out · **easy synonyms:** prevent, stop, block · 🧠 **trick:** pre-CLUDE = to CLOSE the door before (pre) something can happen.
> *A lack of funding need not preclude high-quality research, the editorial insists.*

**Warranted** *(adjective)* — justified by the facts; reasonable · **easy synonyms:** justified, fair, deserved · 🧠 **trick:** WARRANT = a paper that gives official permission — so it is justified.
> *Given the rising case numbers, stricter travel rules now appear warranted.*

**Nuanced** *(adjective)* — showing fine, careful differences and detail · **easy synonyms:** subtle, detailed, careful · 🧠 **trick:** NUANCED = noticing 'new' tiny shades others miss.
> *The journalist offered a nuanced account that resisted easy heroes and villains.*

**Substantiate** *(verb)* — to prove with evidence; to back up a claim · **easy synonyms:** prove, support, back up · 🧠 **trick:** sub-STANCE-iate = give an idea real SUBSTANCE (solid proof).
> *The newspaper could not substantiate the allegations and issued a public correction.*

**Tacit** *(adjective)* — understood without being said openly · **easy synonyms:** unspoken, silent, implied · 🧠 **trick:** TACIT shares a root with 'taciturn' (silent) — agreement kept quiet.
> *By taking no action, the regulator gave tacit approval to the merger.*

**Unequivocal** *(adjective)* — completely clear, leaving no doubt · **easy synonyms:** clear, plain, certain · 🧠 **trick:** un-EQUI-VOCAL = NOT having two (equi) voices (vocal) — only one clear message.
> *The panel issued an unequivocal warning that emissions must fall this decade.*

**Germinal** *(adjective)* — in the earliest stage; a starting point that things grow from · **easy synonyms:** early, beginning, seed-stage · 🧠 **trick:** GERMINAL = like a GERM/seed that everything later grows out of.
> *The 1953 paper is now seen as a germinal work in the field of genetics.*

**Attribute** *(verb)* — to say something is caused by a particular thing · **easy synonyms:** credit, blame, link · 🧠 **trick:** a-TRIBUTE = you 'give credit/tribute' to whatever caused it.
> *The study attributes the fall in crime to better street lighting rather than policing.*

**Hitherto** *(adverb)* — up until now; before this time · **easy synonyms:** so far, until now, previously · 🧠 **trick:** HITHER-TO = 'to here' in time — everything up to this point.
> *The telescope revealed structures hitherto unseen at the edge of the galaxy.*

**Notwithstanding** *(preposition)* — in spite of; despite · **easy synonyms:** despite, even with, regardless of · 🧠 **trick:** NOT-WITH-STANDING = the obstacle 'stands' but does NOT stop you.
> *Notwithstanding the funding cuts, the laboratory completed its trials on schedule.*

**Insofar** *(adverb)* — to the extent that; only as far as · **easy synonyms:** as much as, to the point that, where · 🧠 **trick:** IN-SO-FAR = only 'so far' and no further.
> *The model is useful insofar as it predicts short-term shifts, but it fails over longer periods.*

**Thereby** *(adverb)* — by that means; as a result of that · **easy synonyms:** so, thus, in that way · 🧠 **trick:** THERE-BY = 'by there' — by means of that action.
> *The reforms cut paperwork and thereby freed doctors to see more patients.*

**Albeit** *(conjunction)* — although; even though · **easy synonyms:** though, even if, while · 🧠 **trick:** ALBEIT = 'all-be-it' — let it all be true, even so.
> *Growth returned in the third quarter, albeit at a slower pace than hoped.*

**Tentative** *(adjective)* — not final or sure; just a first try that may change · **easy synonyms:** unsure, temporary, not fixed · 🧠 **trick:** 'tentative' shares a root with 'attempt' (tent- = to try) - it's just a TRY, not certain.
> *Researchers offered only a tentative explanation, warning that more data was needed before any firm conclusion.*

**Presumably** *(adverb)* — probably; this is what we assume without full proof · **easy synonyms:** probably, likely, I assume · 🧠 **trick:** 'presume' = take before proof; 'presumably' = we PRE-assume it's true.
> *The funds were, presumably, redirected to defence, though the budget gave no clear breakdown.*

**Arguably** *(adverb)* — it can be reasonably claimed; one could make a good case for it · **easy synonyms:** possibly, you could say, perhaps · 🧠 **trick:** 'arguably' = you could ARGUE it - a soft way to make a strong claim.
> *This is arguably the most important climate report published in the last decade.*

**Conceivably** *(adverb)* — it is possible to imagine; it could possibly happen · **easy synonyms:** possibly, maybe, it could be · 🧠 **trick:** 'conceive' = to form an idea in the mind; 'conceivably' = you can CONCEIVE (imagine) it.
> *The drug could conceivably be repurposed to treat other inflammatory diseases.*

## 🏛️ Latin Terms & Formal Connectors  *(11 words)*

*The Latin abbreviations and linking words that hold formal writing together. Small but everywhere.*

**i.e.** *(abbreviation)* — Latin 'id est', meaning 'that is' - used to explain or restate what you just said · **easy synonyms:** that is, in other words · 🧠 **trick:** i.e. = 'in essence' - it restates the same thing more clearly.
> *The study focused on adolescents, i.e., children aged 13 to 18.*

**e.g.** *(abbreviation)* — Latin 'exempli gratia', meaning 'for example' - used before examples · **easy synonyms:** for example, such as · 🧠 **trick:** e.g. = 'example given' - both start with the same letters.
> *Several risk factors, e.g., smoking and obesity, were linked to the disease.*

**per capita** *(phrase)* — Latin for 'per head' - for each person, used when comparing countries or groups · **easy synonyms:** per person, for each person · 🧠 **trick:** 'Capita' = head (like 'captain'); per capita = per head/person.
> *Per capita income in the region has doubled over the past two decades.*

**viz.** *(abbreviation)* — stands for Latin 'videlicet' = 'namely'; used to introduce a full, specific list of what you mean · **easy synonyms:** namely, that is, specifically · 🧠 **trick:** viz. = 'VIZualise exactly' which ones — it names the precise items.
> *Only one group showed improvement, viz. the patients who received the higher dose.*

**ibid.** *(abbreviation)* — stands for Latin 'ibidem' = 'in the same place'; used in footnotes to point to the same source just cited · **easy synonyms:** same source, as above, same book · 🧠 **trick:** IBID = 'I Brought It Down again' — same source as the line above.
> *The first figure comes from the 2021 survey; the second is drawn from the same report (ibid., p. 44).*

**cf.** *(abbreviation)* — 'compare' - tells the reader to look at another source for comparison · **easy synonyms:** compare, see also, look at · 🧠 **trick:** cf. = Latin 'confer' = 'compare'; think 'C for compare'.
> *Growth slowed sharply this quarter (cf. the boom of the previous year).*

**prima facie** *(phrase)* — on first look; based on what seems true at first · **easy synonyms:** at first sight, on the surface, apparently · 🧠 **trick:** Latin 'prima facie' = 'first face'; what you see on the FIRST FACE/glance.
> *There is prima facie evidence of fraud, though a full inquiry is still needed.*

**de facto** *(phrase)* — Latin for 'in fact'; existing in reality even if not officially · **easy synonyms:** in practice, actually, in reality · 🧠 **trick:** 'de facto' = by fact -> real in practice, not on paper.
> *With no formal treaty, the ceasefire became the de facto policy along the border.*

**etc.** *(abbreviation)* — 'and so on' - shows a list continues with similar things · **easy synonyms:** and so on, and more · 🧠 **trick:** Latin 'et cetera' = 'and the rest'. Never write 'and etc.'.
> *The survey covered basic services - water, power, sanitation, etc.*

**per se** *(phrase)* — 'in itself' - used to say a thing on its own, not its effects or links · **easy synonyms:** in itself, by itself · 🧠 **trick:** Latin 'by itself'. Think 'per (one) self' = the thing alone.
> *The policy is not harmful per se, but its timing worried investors.*

**Ergo** *(adverb)* — therefore; for that reason (sounds formal or logical) · **easy synonyms:** therefore, so, thus · 🧠 **trick:** Latin 'ergo' = 'therefore'. 'I think, ergo I am.'.
> *Supply fell while demand rose; ergo, prices climbed.*
