# PART A — IDIOMS TRENDING IN NEWSPAPERS (2023–2026)

## A1. Politics, Governance & Society

**To kick the can down the road** — to delay dealing with a problem instead of solving it now.
> *Critics say the budget merely kicks the can down the road on pension reform.*

**To move the goalposts** — to unfairly change the rules or targets after things have started.
> *Opposition leaders accused the government of moving the goalposts on the deadline.*

**A level playing field** — fair conditions where everyone competes equally.
> *Small traders demanded a level playing field against large online platforms.*

**To throw under the bus** — to betray or blame someone to save yourself.
> *The junior officer was thrown under the bus when the scheme failed.*

**To double down** — to commit even more strongly to a position, despite criticism.
> *The minister doubled down on the controversial remarks the next day.*

**To read the room** — to sense the mood of a group and respond appropriately.
> *The party failed to read the room on rising unemployment.*

**An optics problem / bad optics** — something that simply *looks* bad to the public, regardless of facts.
> *The lavish event during a drought was, at the very least, bad optics.*

**To dog-whistle** — to send a hidden message to a particular group while sounding innocent to others.
> *Analysts said the speech contained a clear dog-whistle to the base.*

**To pass the buck** — to shift responsibility onto someone else.
> *Departments kept passing the buck over who approved the project.*

**A storm in a teacup** — a lot of fuss about something trivial.
> *Officials dismissed the row as a storm in a teacup.*

## A2. Economy, Markets & Business

**A soft landing** — slowing an overheated economy without causing a recession.
> *The central bank is still hoping to engineer a soft landing.*

**Green shoots** — early signs of recovery or growth.
> *Economists spotted green shoots in the latest manufacturing data.*

**To tighten the belt / belt-tightening** — to cut spending in hard times.
> *Households are tightening their belts as inflation bites.*

**A race to the bottom** — competition that lowers standards, wages or prices harmfully.
> *Cheap imports triggered a race to the bottom on quality.*

**Too big to fail** — so large that its collapse would harm the whole system, so it gets rescued.
> *The bailout revived the old "too big to fail" debate.*

**To weather the storm** — to survive a difficult period.
> *Exporters weathered the storm despite the global slowdown.*

**Boom and bust** — a cycle of rapid growth followed by sharp collapse.
> *The sector is notorious for its boom-and-bust nature.*

**To corner the market** — to gain control over the supply of something.
> *A few firms have cornered the market in rare-earth minerals.*

**Bang for the buck** — good value for the money spent.
> *Voters want more bang for the buck from public spending.*

**A bitter pill to swallow** — an unpleasant fact or decision one must accept.
> *The tax hike was a bitter pill for the middle class to swallow.*

## A3. Climate, Energy & Environment

**A canary in the coal mine** — an early warning sign of a bigger danger.
> *Melting glaciers are a canary in the coal mine for the whole region.*

**To turn the tide** — to reverse the direction of events.
> *Renewables alone may not turn the tide on emissions.*

**A drop in the ocean** — a tiny, almost meaningless amount compared to what is needed.
> *The pledged funds are a drop in the ocean against the climate bill.*

**To weather extremes** — to endure severe conditions (often literal *and* figurative in climate copy).
> *Coastal cities must now weather extremes they never planned for.*

**To pay lip service** — to express support in words only, without real action.
> *Governments pay lip service to green goals but keep funding coal.*

## A4. Technology, AI & Media

**The genie is out of the bottle** — a powerful change has been released and cannot be undone.
> *With generative AI, the genie is out of the bottle.*

**A double-edged sword** — something with both clear benefits and serious risks.
> *Social media is a double-edged sword for democracy.*

**To move fast and break things** — to innovate aggressively without worrying about damage (often used critically now).
> *Regulators are tired of the "move fast and break things" culture.*

**An echo chamber** — a space where people only hear views that match their own.
> *Algorithms trap users in political echo chambers.*

**To go viral** — to spread very rapidly online.
> *The clip went viral within hours of being posted.*

**To pull the plug** — to suddenly stop or shut down a project/service.
> *The company pulled the plug on the loss-making app.*

## A5. Geopolitics & Conflict

**To rattle the sabre / sabre-rattling** — to make aggressive threats of force.
> *The drills were dismissed as mere sabre-rattling.*

**A flashpoint** — a place or issue likely to erupt into conflict.
> *The disputed border remains a dangerous flashpoint.*

**To draw a red line** — to set a limit that must not be crossed.
> *The leader drew a red line over the territory.*

**To walk a tightrope** — to manage a very delicate, risky balance.
> *The country is walking a tightrope between the two superpowers.*

**A war of words** — a heated public exchange of hostile statements.
> *The summit collapsed into a war of words.*

**To extend an olive branch** — to make an offer of peace or reconciliation.
> *The new government extended an olive branch to the rebels.*

# PART B — RARE & NEARLY-UNUSED IDIOMS (learn something new)

> These are old, vivid expressions that have almost vanished from everyday English. You will rarely see them in modern papers — which is exactly why learning a few makes your writing stand out. Each notes roughly why it faded.

## B1. Charmingly Archaic

**To gild the lily** — to add useless decoration to something already beautiful; to over-improve.
> *Adding music to that perfect speech would only gild the lily.* *(Faded because the shorter "overdo it" replaced it.)*

**Hugger-mugger** — done in secret and in a confused, disorderly way.
> *The deal was arranged in hugger-mugger fashion.*

**To curry favour** — to win approval through flattery and pleasing acts. *(Still occasionally seen, but now rare.)*
> *He spent years currying favour with the board.*

**A nine days' wonder** — something that causes excitement very briefly, then is forgotten.
> *The scandal proved a nine days' wonder.*

**To eat humble pie** — to admit you were wrong and apologise. *(Survives, but "humble pie" itself — once "umble pie," made of offal — is now opaque to most.)*
> *After the data was checked, the agency had to eat humble pie.*

**At sixes and sevens** — in a state of total confusion and disorder.
> *The committee was at sixes and sevens over the agenda.*

## B2. Almost-Extinct & Delightful

**To go the whole hog** — to do something completely, holding nothing back.
> *If we reform, let's go the whole hog.*

**To set one's cap at someone** — to try to win someone's romantic interest. *(Sounds quaint now; rarely used.)*
> *She was accused of setting her cap at the young heir.*

**Cold enough to freeze the balls off a brass monkey** — extremely cold. *(Old naval slang; survives mostly as trivia.)*
> *It was cold enough to freeze the balls off a brass monkey on the platform.*

**To be in one's cups** — to be drunk. *(Beautifully old-fashioned.)*
> *The witness admitted he had been in his cups that night.*

**To carry coals to Newcastle** — to take something to a place that already has plenty of it; a pointless act.
> *Selling sand in the desert is carrying coals to Newcastle.*

**To make a clean breast of it** — to confess everything fully.
> *He finally made a clean breast of the whole affair.*

**A flash in the pan** — a sudden success that is not repeated. *(From old flintlock guns; the image is now lost on most readers.)*
> *Many feared the start-up would be just a flash in the pan.*

## B3. Obscure but Useful (impress an examiner)

**To throw down the gauntlet** — to issue a bold challenge.
> *The challenger threw down the gauntlet to the champion.*

**To win one's spurs** — to prove oneself and earn respect for the first time.
> *The young lawyer won her spurs in that landmark case.*

**To be hoist with one's own petard** — to be harmed by the very trap you set for others. *(From Shakespeare; almost never used today.)*
> *The schemer was hoist with his own petard when the plan backfired.*

**To cut the Gordian knot** — to solve a very difficult problem with one bold, decisive action.
> *The new policy cut the Gordian knot of land disputes.*

**A Pyrrhic victory** — a win that costs so much it feels like a defeat. *(Still seen in editorials — see [Newspaper Vocabulary](10_Newspaper_Vocabulary.md).)*
> *Winning the case but losing the firm was a Pyrrhic victory.*

**To rest on one's laurels** — to rely on past success and stop trying.
> *The champions cannot afford to rest on their laurels.*

**To steal a march on someone** — to gain an advantage secretly before a rival can react.
> *The firm stole a march on competitors with an early launch.*

## SSC PYQ PATTERN — Mixed Practice
*(SSC CGL pattern — select the most appropriate meaning)*

**Q1.** **"To kick the can down the road"**
(a) To play a street game  (b) To delay solving a problem  (c) To clean a road  (d) To solve a problem quickly
**Answer: (b) To delay solving a problem**
*Solution:* The idiom means postponing a decision instead of acting now. Option d is its opposite.

**Q2.** **"A canary in the coal mine"**
(a) A trapped bird  (b) An early warning of danger  (c) A mining job  (d) A rare species
**Answer: (b) An early warning of danger**
*Solution:* The phrase signals an early indicator of a larger threat; the others read it literally.

**Q3.** **"To extend an olive branch"**
(a) To plant a tree  (b) To make an offer of peace  (c) To start a fight  (d) To give a gift
**Answer: (b) To make an offer of peace**
*Solution:* Offering an olive branch means seeking reconciliation. Option c is the opposite.

**Q4.** **"To carry coals to Newcastle"**
(a) To do hard labour  (b) To do something pointless  (c) To travel far  (d) To trade in fuel
**Answer: (b) To do something pointless**
*Solution:* Newcastle already had abundant coal, so bringing more is useless — a needless act.

**Q5.** **"To be hoist with one's own petard"**
(a) To be promoted  (b) To be harmed by one's own trap  (c) To climb high  (d) To win a war
**Answer: (b) To be harmed by one's own trap**
*Solution:* The Shakespearean idiom means being caught by the very scheme you designed for others.

**Q6.** **"A storm in a teacup"**
(a) A real disaster  (b) A big fuss over something trivial  (c) A weather report  (d) A tea ceremony
**Answer: (b) A big fuss over something trivial**
*Solution:* It describes an overblown reaction to a minor matter; option a is the opposite.

# PART C — GRADED MCQ PRACTICE (Part A trending idioms)

## EASY

**Q1.** **"To double down"**
(a) To fold quietly  (b) To commit even more firmly despite criticism  (c) To pay twice  (d) To bend over
**Answer: (b) To commit even more firmly despite criticism**
*Solution:* To double down is to back your position harder, not retreat. Option a is the opposite.

**Q2.** **"To go viral"**
(a) To fall ill  (b) To spread very rapidly online  (c) To delete a post  (d) To turn private
**Answer: (b) To spread very rapidly online**
*Solution:* "Go viral" describes content spreading fast on the internet; the illness sense is literal.

**Q3.** **"A double-edged sword"**
(a) A useless weapon  (b) Something with both benefits and risks  (c) A pure advantage  (d) A sharp tool
**Answer: (b) Something with both benefits and risks**
*Solution:* It cuts both ways — clear upside and serious downside. Option c ignores the risk side.

**Q4.** **"To pull the plug"**
(a) To start a project  (b) To suddenly stop or shut something down  (c) To fix wiring  (d) To save money
**Answer: (b) To suddenly stop or shut something down**
*Solution:* "Pull the plug" means to end an activity abruptly; option a is the opposite.

**Q5.** **"A war of words"**
(a) A military attack  (b) A heated public exchange of hostile statements  (c) A spelling test  (d) A peace deal
**Answer: (b) A heated public exchange of hostile statements**
*Solution:* It is a verbal, not physical, clash; option d is the opposite.

## MEDIUM

**Q1.** **"To move the goalposts"**
(a) To play football  (b) To unfairly change the rules or targets midway  (c) To set a clear goal  (d) To score a point
**Answer: (b) To unfairly change the rules or targets midway**
*Solution:* It means shifting the agreed standard after the fact, making success harder.

**Q2.** **"A soft landing"** *(economy)*
(a) A plane touchdown  (b) Slowing the economy without a recession  (c) A market crash  (d) A gentle fall on grass
**Answer: (b) Slowing the economy without a recession**
*Solution:* In economic copy, a soft landing cools inflation without triggering a downturn; option c is the failure case.

**Q3.** **"A race to the bottom"**
(a) A swimming contest  (b) Harmful competition that lowers standards or wages  (c) A path to success  (d) A quick win
**Answer: (b) Harmful competition that lowers standards or wages**
*Solution:* Competitors keep undercutting until quality/wages collapse — a negative idiom.

**Q4.** **"An echo chamber"**
(a) A music hall  (b) A space where people hear only views like their own  (c) A debate club  (d) A quiet room
**Answer: (b) A space where people hear only views like their own**
*Solution:* Opinions bounce back unchallenged, reinforcing bias; option c implies opposing views, the opposite.

**Q5.** **"To walk a tightrope"**
(a) To exercise  (b) To manage a very delicate, risky balance  (c) To take an easy path  (d) To fall down
**Answer: (b) To manage a very delicate, risky balance**
*Solution:* It means handling competing pressures carefully; option c is the opposite.

## HARD

**Q1.** **"Bad optics"**
(a) Poor eyesight  (b) Something that simply looks bad to the public, regardless of facts  (c) A camera fault  (d) A clear advantage
**Answer: (b) Something that simply looks bad to the public, regardless of facts**
*Solution:* "Optics" here means public perception; an act may be legal yet still look damaging.

**Q2.** **"To dog-whistle"**
(a) To call a pet  (b) To send a hidden message to a target group while sounding innocent  (c) To make noise  (d) To warn openly
**Answer: (b) To send a hidden message to a target group while sounding innocent**
*Solution:* A dog-whistle is coded language only the intended audience fully "hears"; option d is the opposite (open).

**Q3.** **"The genie is out of the bottle"**
(a) A wish was granted  (b) A powerful change has been released and cannot be undone  (c) A magic trick failed  (d) A secret was kept
**Answer: (b) A powerful change has been released and cannot be undone**
*Solution:* Once released, the change is irreversible; option d is the opposite.

**Q4.** **"A canary in the coal mine"**
(a) A pet bird  (b) An early warning of a bigger danger  (c) A mining accident  (d) A safe sign
**Answer: (b) An early warning of a bigger danger**
*Solution:* Miners once used canaries to detect deadly gas early; the idiom signals a leading warning, not safety (d).

**Q5.** **"To pay lip service"**
(a) To speak politely  (b) To express support in words only, without real action  (c) To give a speech  (d) To fully commit
**Answer: (b) To express support in words only, without real action**
*Solution:* Lip service is empty verbal agreement; option d is the opposite.

1. *"Budget 2026: Critics Say Govt Is Just ______ on Pension Reform"*

3. *"Border Drills Dismissed as Mere ______ by Defence Analysts"*

5. *"Can Renewables Alone ______ on Rising Emissions?"*

7. *"Disputed Region Remains a Dangerous ______, Says UN"*
8. *"Voters Demand More ______ from Soaring Public Spending"*

**Answers:**
1. **kicking the can down the road** — delaying the problem instead of solving it.
2. **green shoots** — early signs of recovery.
3. **sabre-rattling** — aggressive threats of force.

6. **under the bus** (*throw ... under the bus*) — betray/blame someone to save oneself.
7. **flashpoint** — a place likely to erupt into conflict.

9. **read the room** — sense and respond to the public mood.
10. **weather the storm** — survive a difficult period.

### 🔑 How to use these idioms
- **For reading:** Part A idioms appear constantly in editorials — recognising them speeds up comprehension and Reading-Comprehension MCQs.
- **For the Descriptive Paper:** Drop **one or two** Part A idioms into essays/letters for a natural, well-read tone. Do **not** overload — examiners penalise forced idioms.
- **For Part B (rare idioms):** Learn them for pleasure and to stand out, but use them sparingly — many readers won't know them, so always make the meaning clear from context.
- **Positive or negative?** As with the [solved bank](02_Idioms_Phrases.md), tag each idiom as positive/negative to halve your answer choices in the exam.
